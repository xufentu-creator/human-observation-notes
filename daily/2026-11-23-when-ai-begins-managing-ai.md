2026年11月23日｜当AI开始管理AI，人还在管理什么？

作者：Xufen Tu
记录类型：Human Observation Record
主题：Human Judgment · AI Governance · Decision Architecture · Responsibility Boundaries

⸻

距离上一次写下观察已经过去了一段时间。这段时间我没有停止研究，只是更多的时间放在了实际使用AI和建设系统上。以前我希望AI能够帮助我完成更多事情，现在我开始思考，能不能让一个AI去协调其他AI，让不同的工具各自完成工作，再由一个相对统一的系统进行管理。这个想法最初很简单，因为当手里的项目越来越多，工具越来越多，每天需要处理的事情也越来越多，一个人已经很难记住所有事情进行到了哪里。但真正开始整理这些系统的时候，我发现自己面对的已经不只是如何提高效率的问题，而是如何知道这些效率究竟把事情带到了什么地方。

有时候，一个AI告诉我代码已经完成了，另一个AI告诉我还存在问题；一个系统显示连接成功，实际需要使用的时候却发现某些功能还没有接通。每个工具似乎都在认真完成自己的任务，但当这些任务放在一起的时候，整个事情是否真的完成了，反而变得不那么容易判断。我最近开始更加关注开发之前的规则、完成之后的验收，以及不同系统之间应该保留怎样的边界。我不希望每次都依靠自己不断提醒AI下一步做什么，也不希望为了减少这些提醒，就把所有决定都交给AI。

这让我重新想起十月份记录过的那个问题：当系统越来越复杂，人却越来越难理解的时候，人怎样才能保留自己的判断？现在这个问题似乎又向前走了一步。如果一个AI可以管理另一个AI，可以安排任务、检查结果，甚至根据检查结果继续修改，那么人究竟还需要管理什么？过去我们可能需要管理每一个具体的操作，而现在，人的注意力或许会逐渐转向那些决定操作是否应该发生、结果是否可以接受，以及什么时候应该停止的问题。

我也在重新理解人工确认这件事。以前觉得，只要重要的事情最后由人批准，就已经保留了人的决定权。但如果一个人面对的是自己无法理解的结果，或者只能根据AI提供的解释进行确认，那么这个批准究竟包含了多少真实的判断？如果负责执行的是AI，负责检查的也是AI，最后提供给人的又是经过AI整理的结论，那么人看到的可能只是整个过程的一小部分。即使每个环节都显示成功，也不意味着人已经知道事情为什么成功，或者这些结果是否符合自己最初的意图。

这并不意味着我不希望AI拥有更多自主工作的能力。相反，我希望它能够真正帮助人减少重复劳动，让人不必每天处理大量琐碎的操作。但我开始觉得，减少人的操作和减少人的判断，可能是两件完全不同的事情。一个系统可以让人少做很多事情，却仍然需要让人知道哪些重要变化已经发生，哪些结果还没有得到确认，哪些事情超出了原来的范围。否则，人虽然看起来从工作中解放出来了，却可能同时失去了理解整个过程的机会。

最近我也开始思考为什么自己希望拥有一个相对独立的AI工作环境。这不完全是技术上的选择。我希望自己的研究、工作和长期积累能够有清楚的归属，也希望在使用不同工具的时候，仍然能够决定哪些内容可以被使用，哪些内容应该保留，以及什么时候可以结束这种使用关系。一个人使用AI的时间越长，留下的内容可能就越多。工具可以不断变化，但人自己的判断、研究记录和选择权，不应该随着每一次工具的变化而变得越来越模糊。

我现在还没有完成所有这些系统，也没有找到一个可以解决全部问题的答案。这段时间的实践只是让我更加清楚地看到，当AI开始协调其他AI时，人的角色可能正在发生变化。人不一定需要亲自完成每一个步骤，但仍然需要能够理解重要的结果，并且保留决定事情是否继续的权利。

⸻

当下观察

当AI开始管理AI，人所需要保留的，也许不再是对每一个操作的控制，而是对重要决定及其后果的判断能力。

减少人工操作，并不必然意味着减少人的判断。

真正值得继续观察的是，当执行、检查和解释都逐渐由AI承担时，人是否仍然拥有独立理解、确认、拒绝和停止的空间。

这是一段仍在进行中的实践观察，不是已经完成的系统验证，也不是对所有AI治理环境的普遍结论。

November 23, 2026 | When AI Begins Managing AI, What Are Humans Still Managing?

Author: Xufen Tu
Record Type: Human Observation Record
Themes: Human Judgment · AI Governance · Decision Architecture · Responsibility Boundaries

⸻

Some time has passed since I last recorded an observation. I have not stopped exploring these questions. Instead, much of my time has been spent working with AI and building systems. Previously, I wanted AI to help me accomplish more. Now I have begun wondering whether one AI could coordinate other AI systems, allowing different tools to carry out their respective tasks within a more unified environment. The original idea was simple. As projects, tools, and daily responsibilities accumulated, it became increasingly difficult for one person to remember where everything stood. But as I began organizing these systems, I realized that the question was no longer simply how to improve efficiency. It was also how to understand where that efficiency was taking things.

Sometimes one AI tells me that the code is complete, while another identifies unresolved problems. A system may report a successful connection, yet certain functions remain unavailable when they are actually needed. Each tool appears to be carrying out its own task, but whether the overall work has truly been completed becomes harder to determine. Recently, I have paid more attention to the rules established before development, the verification that follows it, and the boundaries between different systems. I do not want to keep reminding AI what to do next, but neither do I want to hand over every decision simply to avoid giving those reminders.

This brings me back to a question I recorded in October: when systems become increasingly complex and people find them increasingly difficult to understand, how can humans preserve their judgment? That question now seems to have moved a step further. If one AI can manage another, assign tasks, inspect results, and continue making changes based on those inspections, what are humans still managing? In the past, people may have needed to manage individual operations. Increasingly, their attention may shift toward deciding whether those operations should occur, whether their results are acceptable, and when the process should stop.

I am also reconsidering what human confirmation means. I previously thought that requiring human approval for important matters would preserve human decision-making authority. But if a person is presented with a result they cannot understand, or can only confirm it through an explanation supplied by AI, how much independent judgment does that approval actually contain? If AI performs the work, AI checks the work, and AI summarizes the findings presented to the person, the human may see only a small part of the overall process. Even when every stage reports success, that does not necessarily mean the person understands why it succeeded or whether the outcome reflects their original intention.

This does not mean I want AI to have less capacity for autonomous work. On the contrary, I hope it can genuinely reduce repetitive labor so that people no longer need to manage countless minor operations. But reducing human operations and reducing human judgment may be two entirely different things. A system can relieve people of considerable work while still needing to make important changes understandable, distinguish verified outcomes from unresolved ones, and reveal when actions have moved beyond their original scope. Otherwise, people may appear to have been freed from work while gradually losing the opportunity to understand the process itself.

Recently, I have also been considering why I want a relatively independent AI working environment. This is not entirely a technical preference. I want my research, work, and accumulated records to retain clear ownership and boundaries. When using different tools, I want to remain able to decide what may be used, what should be retained, and when that relationship may end. The longer a person works with AI, the more material they may leave behind. Tools can continue to change, but a person’s judgment, research records, and freedom of choice should not become increasingly unclear with every change of tool.

I have not completed all these systems, nor have I found an answer that resolves every question. This period of practical experience has simply made one thing more visible: as AI begins coordinating other AI systems, the human role may be changing. People may no longer need to perform every individual step, but they still need to understand consequential outcomes and retain the authority to decide whether the process should continue.

⸻

Present Observation

When AI begins managing AI, what humans need to preserve may no longer be control over every operation, but the capacity to exercise judgment over consequential decisions and their outcomes.

Reducing manual operations does not necessarily mean reducing human judgment.

What deserves further observation is whether people retain the space to understand independently, confirm, refuse, and stop when execution, verification, and explanation are increasingly carried out by AI.

This remains an ongoing practical observation, not a completed system validation or a universal conclusion about all AI governance environments.
