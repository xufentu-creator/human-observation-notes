2026年9月8日｜AI 时代，人为什么不能只等系统安排

作者： Xufen Tu
记录类型： Human Observation Record
主题： Human Judgment · Human Agency · AI-mediated Decisions

⸻

今天我继续想到一个很现实的问题：当 AI 和各种系统越来越会安排任务、提醒步骤、生成答案、规划路线以后，人会不会越来越习惯等系统告诉自己下一步做什么？

过去，一个人如果没有方向，可能是等别人安排。等老板安排，等家人安排，等伴侣安排，等老师安排，等组织安排。有人告诉他今天要做什么、几点完成、下一步怎么做，他就可以照着走。这样的状态在很多工作和生活里都很常见，并不一定说明一个人没有能力。很多人执行力不错，也愿意配合，只是他们很少主动问自己：如果没有人提醒，我还会不会继续行动？

AI 出现以后，这个问题变得更明显。因为系统不只是提醒人，还开始替人整理、替人判断、替人规划。它可以告诉你今天该做什么，帮你写一封邮件，帮你总结一段资料，帮你比较几个选择，甚至帮你生成一个看起来很完整的方案。表面上看，人变得更轻松了。可是如果一个人长期习惯被系统推着走，他可能会慢慢失去自己形成方向的能力。

这不是说人不应该使用 AI。AI 可以节省时间，可以帮助人看到更多信息，可以减少很多重复劳动。问题在于，系统给出的下一步，并不等于人自己真的完成了判断。一个任务被排好了，不代表这个任务真的重要；一个方案被生成了，不代表这个方案适合现实；一个提醒出现了，不代表人知道为什么要做；一个流程很顺，不代表方向是对的。

我越来越觉得，AI 时代最容易被忽略的风险，不是人完全不行动，而是人只在被安排时行动。系统提示，他就做；系统没有提示，他就停。AI 给答案，他就采用；AI 没有给答案，他就不知道怎么继续。久而久之，人会变成一个很高效的执行节点，却不再是一个有方向感的人。

这和生活里的关系也很像。一个人如果总是等别人安排，长期相处就会很累。因为真正消耗人的，不只是多做了一件事，而是总有一个人必须先发现问题、先判断轻重、先安排下一步、先承担没有答案时的压力。另一方可能并不坏，也可能愿意配合，但如果他一直等待指令，关系里就会慢慢出现责任不平衡。陪伴会变成管理，合作会变成单方推动。

AI 系统也可能让这种状态扩大。过去，一个人依赖另一个人给方向；未来，一个人可能依赖系统给方向。看起来他每天都在行动，实际上他的行动越来越来自外部提示。这样的人不一定懒，也不一定没有知识，但他可能越来越少练习一种很重要的能力：在没有安排的时候，自己判断什么值得做，什么不能再拖，什么需要暂停，什么必须继续。

人类判断不是只在重大决策里才重要。它也存在于日常生活最普通的地方。今天先处理哪件事，遇到不确定要不要继续，别人没有提醒时自己能不能看见问题，系统给出建议时自己是否知道哪里需要再想一下，这些都是判断。一个人如果只会执行明确任务，却不能在模糊环境里形成下一步，他的行动就很容易被外部结构决定。

这也是为什么我觉得 human agency 在 AI 时代会越来越重要。人的自主性不是拒绝工具，也不是所有事情都自己手工完成。真正的自主性，是一个人在使用工具的时候，仍然知道自己为什么这样做。AI 可以提供选项，但人要知道自己为什么选择；系统可以安排流程，但人要知道这个流程是否应该继续；工具可以提醒下一步，但人要保留问一句“这真的是现在最重要的吗”的能力。

如果一个人完全依赖系统安排，他可能会失去对现实的敏感。因为现实不总是按照系统提示出现。很多风险没有提醒，很多关系变化没有通知，很多机会不会自动弹出，很多责任也不会被系统清楚标记。真正成熟的人，不只是完成被分配的任务，也能在没有提示的时候看见现实正在变化。

这件事也让我想到企业和社会。很多企业现在希望通过 automation 提高效率，把流程、客服、审批、营销、报告和内部管理都变得更快。可是如果所有人都只是等系统推送任务，那么企业表面上可能很高效，实际却越来越缺少主动判断。真正关键的时刻，可能没有人问：这个流程是否应该停一下？这个结果是否真的适合客户？这个决定会不会产生责任？这个自动化有没有越过边界？

AI 越强，人越不能把自己缩小成等待指令的角色。因为机器已经很擅长执行、生成、排序、提醒和推动。如果人也只剩下执行，那么人和机器之间的差别会越来越小。人的价值不在于比系统更快，而在于能够在复杂现实里形成方向、理解后果、保留暂停、承担责任，并在没有人安排的时候仍然知道为什么继续行动。

这不是一个容易的要求。因为现代生活已经让很多人很疲惫。很多人每天被账单、家庭、工作、身份、健康和各种压力推着走，能够完成眼前任务已经很不容易。要求每个人都持续主动判断，听起来也很沉重。所以这个问题不能被简单理解成“谁不主动谁就不好”。更准确地说，是这个时代需要重新重视人的自主判断能力，而不是让系统把所有下一步都安排好。

我现在的判断是：AI 可以帮助人行动，但不能替人产生生命方向。系统可以安排任务，但不能替人决定什么值得。工具可以给出路线，但不能替人承担选择后的后果。人可以借助 AI，但不能只等 AI。一个真正有自主性的人，不是永远拒绝安排，而是在被安排之外，仍然保留自己判断和继续行动的能力。

今天留下的问题是：当 AI 和系统越来越会安排下一步时，人类怎样才能不只成为高效的执行者，而是在没有提示、没有催促、没有现成答案的时候，仍然保留自己的方向感、判断力和行动能力？？

September 8, 2026 | Why Humans Cannot Only Wait for Systems to Arrange the Next Step in the AI Era

Today I continued thinking about a very real question: as AI and systems become increasingly capable of arranging tasks, reminding people of steps, generating answers, and planning routes, will people become more accustomed to waiting for systems to tell them what to do next?

In the past, when a person lacked direction, they might wait for another person to arrange things. They might wait for a boss, a family member, a partner, a teacher, or an organization. If someone told them what to do today, when to finish it, and what the next step should be, they could follow along. This state is common in many areas of work and life, and it does not necessarily mean a person has no ability. Many people have good execution and are willing to cooperate. They simply rarely ask themselves: if no one reminds me, will I still continue to act?

After AI appeared, this problem became more visible. Systems no longer only remind people. They begin to organize, judge, and plan on people’s behalf. AI can tell you what to do today, help you write an email, summarize a document, compare several options, and even generate a plan that looks complete. On the surface, human beings seem to become lighter. But if a person becomes used to being pushed forward by systems for too long, they may gradually lose the ability to form direction by themselves.

This does not mean people should not use AI. AI can save time, help people see more information, and reduce repetitive labor. The issue is that a next step given by a system is not the same as a judgment formed by a human being. A task being arranged does not mean the task is truly important. A plan being generated does not mean the plan fits reality. A reminder appearing does not mean the person knows why they should act. A smooth workflow does not mean the direction is correct.

I increasingly feel that one of the most easily overlooked risks in the AI era is not that people completely stop acting, but that people only act when they are arranged. When the system prompts them, they act. When there is no prompt, they stop. When AI gives an answer, they use it. When AI does not give an answer, they do not know how to continue. Over time, a person may become a very efficient execution node, but no longer a person with a sense of direction.

This is similar to relationships in daily life. If one person always waits for another person to arrange things, long-term life becomes exhausting. What consumes people most is not only doing one extra task, but the fact that one person must always discover the problem first, judge its importance first, arrange the next step first, and carry the pressure of uncertainty first. The other person may not be bad, and may even be willing to cooperate. But if they are always waiting for instructions, responsibility inside the relationship slowly becomes unbalanced. Companionship can turn into management, and cooperation can become one-sided pushing.

AI systems may expand this condition. In the past, a person might depend on another person to provide direction. In the future, a person may depend on systems to provide direction. It may look as if they are acting every day, but their actions increasingly come from external prompts. Such a person may not be lazy, and may not lack knowledge, but they may practice less and less of an important ability: when no one arranges things, to judge for themselves what is worth doing, what cannot be delayed, what needs to pause, and what must continue.

Human judgment is not important only in major decisions. It also exists in the most ordinary parts of daily life. Which thing should be handled first today, whether to continue when uncertainty appears, whether one can see problems without reminders, and whether one knows where to think again when a system gives a recommendation — all of these are judgments. If a person can only execute clear tasks but cannot form a next step in an unclear environment, their actions can easily become determined by external structures.

This is why I feel that human agency will become increasingly important in the AI era. Autonomy does not mean rejecting tools, nor does it mean doing everything manually. Real autonomy means that when using tools, a person still knows why they are doing what they are doing. AI can provide options, but humans need to know why they choose. Systems can arrange workflows, but humans need to know whether the workflow should continue. Tools can remind people of the next step, but people need to preserve the ability to ask, “Is this really the most important thing now?”

If a person completely depends on systems for arrangement, they may lose sensitivity to reality. Reality does not always appear according to system prompts. Many risks have no reminders. Many relationship changes have no notification. Many opportunities do not automatically appear. Many responsibilities are not clearly marked by systems. A mature person is not only someone who completes assigned tasks, but someone who can see that reality is changing even when there is no prompt.

This also makes me think about enterprises and society. Many companies now hope to improve efficiency through automation, making workflows, customer service, approvals, marketing, reporting, and internal management faster. But if everyone only waits for systems to push tasks, an enterprise may appear efficient on the surface while becoming increasingly short of active judgment underneath. At truly critical moments, there may be no one asking: should this process stop for a moment? Does this result truly fit the customer? Will this decision create responsibility? Has this automation crossed a boundary?

The stronger AI becomes, the less humans should shrink themselves into the role of waiting for instructions. Machines are already strong at execution, generation, ranking, reminding, and pushing. If human beings also remain only as executors, the difference between humans and machines will become smaller. Human value does not lie in being faster than systems. It lies in forming direction in complex reality, understanding consequences, preserving the ability to pause, taking responsibility, and still knowing why one continues to act when no one arranges things.

This is not an easy expectation. Modern life has already made many people exhausted. Many people are pushed forward every day by bills, family, work, status, health, and all kinds of pressure. Completing the tasks immediately in front of them is already difficult. Asking every person to constantly judge actively may sound heavy. So this issue should not be simplified into “people who are not proactive are bad.” More accurately, this era needs to value human autonomous judgment again, rather than allowing systems to arrange every next step for people.

My current judgment is this: AI can help people act, but it cannot produce a life direction on their behalf. Systems can arrange tasks, but they cannot decide for people what is worth doing. Tools can provide routes, but they cannot bear the consequences of choices for human beings. People can use AI, but they cannot only wait for AI. A truly autonomous person is not someone who always rejects arrangement, but someone who still preserves their own judgment and ability to continue acting beyond what has been arranged.

The question I am left with today is this: when AI and systems become increasingly capable of arranging the next step, how can human beings avoid becoming only efficient executors, and instead preserve their own sense of direction, judgment, and ability to act when there is no prompt, no urging, and no ready answer?
