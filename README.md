今天，我正式发布了新的研究论文：

**《连续主体与非自动责任：高复制人工智能系统中的身份、判断与归责边界》**

英文标题是：

**The Continuous Subject and Non-Automatic Responsibility: Boundaries of Identity, Judgment, and the Attribution of Responsibility in High-Replication AI Systems**

这篇论文现在拥有了独立 DOI：

**https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21632567**

版本为 **1.0**，中英文两个学术版本被放在同一条正式记录中。对我来说，这不仅仅是把两个文件上传到一个公开平台，也不仅仅是增加了一个可以被引用的链接。真正重要的是，一项已经在私下反复整理、核对和修正的研究，终于获得了一个清楚、稳定、能够被外界辨认的位置。

发布的过程比想象中更需要判断。标题是否完整，中文标题究竟属于别名还是翻译标题，作者姓名和 ORCID 是否对应，文件名是否真正属于这篇论文，许可证允许别人做到什么程度，前序论文之间应当怎样建立引用关系，这些看起来都只是页面里的字段，却会直接影响一项研究以后如何被理解、引用和传播。

今天也再次让我看到，数字系统中的“记录”并不是中性的。一个错误的姓名、一个属于另一篇论文的文件名、一个不准确的许可证，或者一个错误的作者标识，都可能在后续平台中不断复制。系统可以非常快地保存和传播记录，但它不会自动知道这些记录是否真正表达了作者当下的意思。

这也是这篇论文试图澄清的核心问题之一。

主体、来源、版本、语境和决策历史可以保持连续，但知情、意图、授权、控制、同意和责任，不能因为记录仍然存在，就被自动延伸。一个人的姓名出现在某个文件里，不等于这个人已经理解后来发生的一切；一个过去的表达被保存下来，不等于它自动授权新的用途；一个人曾经出现在某个流程中，也不等于所有后续结果都可以自动归到这个人身上。

这并不是说责任永远不存在，也不是说人类在任何情况下都不需要承担责任。真正需要保留的是一个更基本的边界：责任不能仅凭姓名、记录、文件、历史批准或流程痕迹自动成立。是否涉及责任，仍然需要结合真实知情、具体语境、实际控制与明确确认分别判断。

今天的发布过程本身，也让我重新理解了“人类判断”为什么不能被省略。系统可以生成 DOI，可以保存文件，可以同步作者信息，可以建立引用关系，但真正决定哪个文件属于这篇论文、哪个名字属于作者、哪一种许可证符合作者意愿、哪一个版本可以正式公开的，仍然需要人来确认。

这种确认并不一定宏大。很多时候，它只是停下来重新看一遍标题，检查一次 ORCID，发现文件名不对后及时修正，或者拒绝让一个错误记录继续向下复制。但正是这些看起来很小的判断，决定了一项研究最后以什么样的形式进入公共环境。

我也越来越清楚，公开研究并不意味着必须公开所有形成过程。公开页面可以保存正式标题、作者、版本、文件、DOI 和引用关系；完整的研究形成过程、内部修订、结构推导与未公开材料，则继续保留在私有研究档案中。公开的作用是让成果能够被稳定识别，而不是把所有内部工作全部暴露出来。

这次发布所固定的，不只是一个论文链接，也是一条更清楚的研究连续性记录：

对我来说，一项研究真正完成公开，并不是文件出现在互联网上的那一刻，而是它的作者、标题、版本、边界和引用关系终于能够彼此对应，不再依赖模糊记忆，也不再轻易被错误记录替代。

这篇论文今天获得了自己的公开位置。

但记录仍然只是记录。

它可以支持核验，可以帮助引用，可以保存版本连续性，却不能替代真实知情、明确授权、人类判断和具体语境中的责任确认。

# July 27, 2026｜Personal Observation Journal: When a Paper Finally Gains Its Own Public Place

Today, I formally released a new research paper:

**The Continuous Subject and Non-Automatic Responsibility: Boundaries of Identity, Judgment, and the Attribution of Responsibility in High-Replication AI Systems**

Chinese title:

**《连续主体与非自动责任：高复制人工智能系统中的身份、判断与归责边界》**

The paper now has its own DOI:

**https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21632567**

Version **1.0** includes two academic language editions, English and Chinese, within the same formal record. For me, this was not simply a matter of uploading two files or adding another link that can be cited. What mattered was that a body of research that had been repeatedly reviewed, corrected, and organized in private finally gained a clear and stable public location.

The release process required more judgment than it first appeared to. Whether the title was complete, whether the Chinese title should be classified as an alternative or translated title, whether the author name matched the correct ORCID, whether the filenames actually belonged to this paper, what the license allowed others to do, and how earlier papers should be connected through citations all appeared to be ordinary metadata fields. Yet each of them could shape how the research would later be understood, cited, and circulated.

Today again showed me that records in digital systems are not neutral. An incorrect name, a filename belonging to another paper, an unsuitable license, or an inaccurate author identifier may continue to replicate across later platforms. A system can preserve and distribute records very quickly, but it does not automatically know whether those records still represent the author’s actual meaning.

This is also one of the central questions addressed by the paper.

Continuity of subject, source, version, context, and decision history may be preserved. Knowledge, intention, authorization, control, consent, and responsibility, however, must not be extended automatically merely because a record continues to exist. A person’s name appearing in a file does not mean that the person understood everything that happened later. A past expression does not automatically authorize a new use. Prior presence in a process does not automatically attach every later outcome to the same individual.

This does not mean that responsibility never exists, or that human beings never bear responsibility. It establishes a more basic boundary: responsibility must not arise automatically from a name, a record, a file, a prior approval, or a workflow trace alone. Any conclusion concerning responsibility still requires separate consideration of actual knowledge, specific context, practical control, and explicit confirmation.

The release process itself also reminded me why human judgment cannot simply be removed. A system can generate a DOI, preserve files, synchronize author information, and connect citations. But deciding which files belong to the paper, which identity belongs to the author, which license reflects the author’s intention, and which version is ready for public release still requires human confirmation.

Such confirmation does not always appear dramatic. Sometimes it means pausing to read the title again, checking an ORCID, correcting a filename before an error continues to replicate, or refusing to let an inaccurate record move further downstream. Yet these small acts of judgment shape how research enters the public environment.

I have also become clearer that public research does not require every part of its formation process to become public. A public record can preserve the formal title, author, version, files, DOI, and citation relationship. The complete development history, internal revisions, structural reasoning, and non-public materials can remain within a private research archive. The purpose of public release is to make a work stably identifiable, not to expose every internal process behind it.

This release records more than a paper link. It also establishes a clearer line of research continuity:

**Xufen Tu / 涂绪芬**  
**ORCID: 0009-0007-5265-964X**  
**Author Identity ENS: xufentu.eth**  
**Research Continuity Anchor: TUX-133.144~**  
**Version: 1.0**

For me, a research work does not become publicly complete merely when a file appears online. It becomes publicly coherent when its author, title, version, boundaries, and citation relationships finally correspond to one another without depending on uncertain memory or being easily displaced by inaccurate records.

Today, this paper gained its own public place.

But a record remains a record.

It can support verification, citation, and version continuity. It cannot replace actual knowledge, explicit authorization, human judgment, or context-specific confirmation of responsibility.
